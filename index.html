Learn Critical Thinking Competency (CTC)

CTC means questioning claims, evidence, assumptions and framings — including your own. Students separate evidence from assertion, examine how a claim was measured, question the framing itself, and reach a judgement appropriate to what the evidence can support.

The critical shift: from evaluating the answer to interrogating the question.
Source basis: UPM ESD Guidebook and the CTC SIM used for this Studio.

The E-F-O-J Design Language

E — Evidence

Can the evidence support the claim?

Evaluate the source, method and limits of evidence.

F — Framing & Assumptions

What is being taken for granted?

Identify an assumption and what the framing makes less visible.

O — Own Position

What is the weakest point in my conclusion?

Turn critical scrutiny onto your own position.

J — Warranted Judgement

What can I reasonably conclude?

Reach a conclusion proportionate to the quality and limits of evidence.

One claim, four CTC questions

Claim: “Online learning is more environmentally sustainable than face-to-face learning.”
DimensionCTC question
E — EvidenceHow was environmental impact measured? Were devices, data-centre energy and transport included?
F — FramingIs sustainability being defined only through carbon emissions? What impacts become less visible?
O — Own PositionIf I conclude that online learning has lower emissions, what is the weakest part of that conclusion?
J — JudgementDoes the evidence justify “always”, “usually”, or only “under specified conditions”?

What CTC is NOT

Recall ≠ CTC
Reproducing information does not show that the evidence was interrogated.
Opinion ≠ CTC
“I agree” or “I disagree” may remain unsupported.
Scepticism ≠ CTC
Rejecting everything is not critical reasoning; scrutiny must also turn onto one’s own position.
“Critically discuss” ≠ automatically CTC
The task must specify the reasoning students must perform.
Important: We assess the quality and proportionality of reasoning, not whether students agree with the lecturer.

CTC or Not Yet CTC?

1. “List three advantages of solar energy.”
Correct: this mainly measures recall or description.
2. “Do you support solar farms?”
Correct: a personal preference alone is not evidence of CTC.
3. “Evaluate the evidence supporting the claim that solar farms are environmentally sustainable. Identify one assumption in the comparison and the weakest point in your conclusion.”
Correct: the task makes Evidence, Framing, Own Position and Warranted Judgement visible.

CTC versus neighbouring ESD competencies

CompetencyMain question
Critical ThinkingIs this claim, evidence or framing supportable?
Systems ThinkingHow do the parts interact across the system?
NormativeWhat is desirable, fair or ethically appropriate, and for whom?
AnticipatoryWhat futures may emerge?
StrategicHow can change be achieved?
CollaborationHow do we integrate people and perspectives across difference?
Self-awarenessHow do my role, values and actions affect the situation?
Integrated Problem-solvingWhich competencies should be combined to produce a workable response?
Critical vs Normative: Critical asks whether a claim is supportable. Normative asks whether acting on it is right, and for whom.

Mini CTC Simulation

Claim: “Electric vehicles are environmentally sustainable because they have zero tailpipe emissions.”

Source A

Manufacturer advertisement: “Zero emissions while driving.”

Source B

Lifecycle summary: manufacturing emissions are higher than for a conventional vehicle.

Source C

Electricity-mix data: charging emissions vary substantially by electricity source.

Try E-F-O-J before opening the indicative reasoning

E: Source A supports only the absence of tailpipe emissions; it does not establish whole-lifecycle sustainability. Sources B and C broaden the evidence base.

F: The phrase “zero emissions” frames the problem around tailpipe emissions and makes manufacturing and electricity generation less visible.

O: A pro-EV conclusion may still be weak if it assumes a low-carbon electricity mix or ignores manufacturing impacts.

J: A proportionate conclusion is that EVs can reduce some emissions under specified lifecycle and electricity conditions, not that they are universally “zero-emission” vehicles.

How to Use CTC Studio

CTC Studio is both a learning environment and a course-design tool. You may start by learning CTC, build a course from Stage 01, or diagnose an assessment you already have.

New to CTCGo to Learn CTC: definition → E-F-O-J → misconceptions → mini simulation → readiness check.

Common mistakes to avoid

Starting from the SDG list
Start from existing disciplinary content, then identify the authentic sustainability issue and Primary SDG.
Using “critically” as the only CTC instruction
Specify what students must do with evidence, framing, their own position and judgement.
Discussion with no visible output
Create an individual written, oral, annotated or process output that can be assessed.
Writing the rubric before the task
Define the assessment evidence first; then create the rubric to judge that evidence.
